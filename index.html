I am a theoretical physicist working in quantum information, many-body physics and the foundations of quantum theory. Since September 2026 I have been a Ramón y Cajal Fellow at the Instituto de Física Corpuscular (IFIC), CSIC – Universitat de València.

Before joining IFIC, I built and led a research group at the Atominstitut of TU Wien as principal investigator of two projects funded by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF), following postdoctoral positions at IQOQI Vienna and at the University of the Basque Country, where I also completed my PhD.

Research

My research lies at the intersection of quantum foundations, quantum information, many-body physics, quantum sensing and metrology, and quantum thermodynamics, and explores the connections between them. The main underlying theme is quantum correlations, both between the parts of a system and between measurements made at different times. I investigate them as resources and distinguish them from classical correlations, with applications to many-body physics, thermodynamics and metrology.

Many-body physics and phases of matter

In systems of many particles, entanglement is hard to pin down: individual particles can rarely be addressed, and often only a few collective quantities can be measured. I develop methods to detect and quantify entanglement from such data, notably generalizations of the spin-squeezing inequalities to particles with many internal levels, and use them to study how entanglement changes across phases of matter and with temperature. For example, in a model of spins that all interact with each other, we found that entanglement can appear at nonzero temperature even in an ordered phase whose ground state is separable. Much of this work is done in close contact with experiments on ultracold atoms.

Thermodynamic laws and transformations in the quantum regime

The laws of thermodynamics limit how much work can be extracted from heat, what it costs to erase information, and how close to absolute zero a system can be cooled. What these limits become for quantum systems, where correlations can be both a resource and a cost, is still being clarified. I study the energy, time and control complexity needed to cool a quantum system, the work needed to correlate its parts, and how entanglement is created and transformed by elementary thermodynamic operations on many-body systems, such as the compression of a one-dimensional Bose gas. These questions lead towards thermal machines made of quantum fields, which could be realized with ultracold atoms.

Quantum metrology

Quantum correlations allow sensors and clocks to go beyond the precision attainable with classical resources. I study which correlations are responsible for this advantage and how large it can be, using tools such as the quantum Fisher information and uncertainty relations, with a particular focus on estimating several parameters at the same time. With experimental colleagues, we recently used pairs of entangled three-level strontium atoms to estimate two phases on two optical clock transitions simultaneously. I also study timekeeping itself: how precisely a device with finite memory can tick, and when quantum devices outperform classical ones with the same memory.
